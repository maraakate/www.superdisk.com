New Survey Confirms Consumers Are Concerned About Technology Obsolescence

OAKDALE, Minn. (May 12, 1998) - Betamaxes in the basement, 8-tracks in the attic, 286s as decorative planters. Consumers are never quite sure whether a new technology will become a permanent feature in their homes or offices - or at their yard sales. But a recent survey reveals that personal computer users have no desire to turn their computer diskettes into Frisbees or drink coasters.

In a telephone survey of 1,000 consumers conducted by Caravan Opinion Research Corporation International for Imation Corp. (NYSE: IMN), 52 percent of respondents said they have purchased a technology product that can no longer be used today because the manufacturer either stopped making the product or a component of the product.

Forty-seven percent said they were inconvenienced by the practice, and 83 percent said they believe that consumer technology products are becoming obsolete at a faster rate today than in the past.

In recent years, an increase in the use of complex graphic images and large-capacity data files by both business and home personal computer users has sent manufacturers scurrying to develop and market a cost-effective, high-capacity storage solution. Early products on the market have offered proprietary add-on drives and data cartridges that are incompatible with the 1.44 megabyte diskettes that most people currently use.

However, 91 percent of survey respondents said that, given the choice, they would prefer a disk drive that offers the option to use both higher-capacity diskettes and their existing 1.44 megabyte diskettes. Only 5 percent said they would prefer a disk drive that uses higher-capacity diskettes, but discontinues use of the lower-capacity option. (Four percent said they did not know which option they would prefer).

"Computer users are clear in their preferences. They want to be able to store larger text and graphic files and recognize they need more capacity than a 1.44 megabyte diskette can provide. At the same time, they want to be able to continue to use their existing floppy diskettes, because thats where their files are stored," said Ray Meifert, SuperDisk business director at Imation.

"Theyre saying, I need more capacity to store presentations and graphics, but Id also like to continue to use what Im accustomed to using," Meifert added. "We believe that today, and for some time to come, the solution is a combination of both existing and high-capacity diskettes that can be jointly used in a single drive bay."

The survey also revealed that only 8 percent of consumers describe themselves as "quick to adopt" to new technology. At the same time, 45 percent of respondents called themselves "slow to adopt, or among the last to purchase the latest technology products," and 44 percent said they tend to "watch and wait" to see what others buy before deciding to make a technology purchase.

In response to both consumer preference and the buying habits of technology purchasers, Imation and a growing number of computer manufacturers now offer SuperDisk drives and diskettes. Computer users who specify SuperDisk when they buy a new desktop or notebook PC, or who upgrade their current a: drive to a SuperDisk drive, can save large files on the 120 megabyte SuperDisk diskette while also saving smaller files on 1.44 diskettes, all in the same drive.

Compatibility with existing diskettes and ease of use - not to mention the ability to store 83 times more than can be stored on a 1.44 megabyte diskette - make SuperDisk the logical choice for the 89 percent of consumers who say they are more cautious about adopting a new technology. SuperDisk technology, which also is available as an external parallel port drive, is the only high-capacity storage solution currently on the market that works with both 120 megabyte SuperDisk diskettes and 1.44 megabyte diskettes in the same drive.

Now, many of the worlds most respected computer manufacturers have announced plans to offer SuperDisk drives in place of standard 1.44 megabyte diskette drives as a standard or optional component in new models of desktop and notebook computers. These companies include Compaq, NEC, Hewlett-Packard, Acer, Gateway 2000 and many more.

The introduction of SuperDisk as a built-in drive in notebooks means business users - particularly those who travel - can now store and transfer large presentation files without shouldering the weight of additional external drives and incompatible cartridges.

"Like internal modems, this is another step toward making the lives of business travelers more convenient - and less painful," said Meifert.

The survey consisted of telephone interviews conducted among a national probability sample of 1,012 adults comprised of 504 men and 508 women age 18 and older. Percentage results have an error rate of plus or minus 3 percent.
